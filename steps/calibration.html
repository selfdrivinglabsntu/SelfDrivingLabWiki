Step 06

Calibration

Tune volumes and coordinates so every draw is accurate.

In progress

On this page

    What you are calibrating

    • One anchor coordinate for every item on the bed plate — stored in points.yaml
    • The grid geometry and working depths of each item model — stored in equipment.yaml
    • Which item sits on which anchor, and what role it plays — stored in preset.yaml
    • How long the actuator runs per microlitre — stored in printers.yaml

    For calibrating the 3D printer you do not need to manually enter the coordinates for every tip, bottle or well on the bed. Instead, each item on the bed plate is defined by a single anchor coordinate and its geometry. The anchor coordinate is the reference position of the grid — typically the centre of its first well or vessel, depending on which corner is selected as the starting point. The GUI uses this reference position, together with the grid geometry, to calculate the coordinates of every other position automatically.

    The geometry is essentially a grid. Whether it’s a tip rack, bottle holder or well plate, each is treated as a set of evenly spaced rows and columns. To define one, you only need to specify the number of rows and columns, the centre-to-centre spacing (pitch), which corner serves as the anchor point, and how far the pipette should descend when accessing it.

    Once these parameters are set correctly, the GUI can work out where each tip, bottle or well is located. This allows the pipette to pick up the correct tip, reach the stock solution just below its surface, and dispense into the intended well without having to manually define every position.

    All these settings are stored in four YAML files under GUI/configs. Since YAML files are plain text, you can configure the values through the GUI and still inspect, compare, version-control or edit them manually whenever needed.

    0. How the coordinate model works

    Before editing anything, it is worth knowing which file holds what — most calibration problems turn out to be a correct number in the wrong file.

    0.1. The four configuration files

    FileWhat it storesScopeNormally written by
    points.yaml Named x / y / z anchor coordinates, in millimetres One entry per physical position on the deck Configure Plate → Set → Save
    equipment.yaml Grid size, pitch, anchor corner and working depths One entry per equipment model, reusable on any deck By hand
    preset.yaml Which model sits on which anchor, and its role One entry per bed-plate layout Configure Plate → Save Preset
    printers.yaml Pipette limits, actuation timings, tip disposal, default preset One entry per handler By hand — see Software §1

    The split matters. equipment.yaml describes what a piece of labware is and travels with the design; points.yaml describes where you happened to put it and is specific to your machine. Two HEIMDALLs can share an equipment definition and still have completely different points.

    0.2. One anchor, every position

    Each item is treated as a regular grid. The GUI takes the anchor from points.yaml, the pitch and grid size from equipment.yaml, and generates the rest:

    x = anchor.x + column × dx
y = anchor.y + row    × dy
z = anchor.z          # every position on an item shares one Z

    dx and dy are the model’s x and y pitch, signed by its origin key — which names the corner of the grid the anchor sits on:

    originColumn step dxRow step dy
    top-left (used when the key is absent)+x−y
    top-right−x−y
    bottom-left+x+y
    bottom-right−x+y

    So a 96-wellplate — origin: bottom-left, 9 mm pitch, 12 rows by 8 columns — anchored at x: 41.9, y: 101.7 covers 41.9–104.9 mm in X and 101.7–200.7 mm in Y: the 63 × 99 mm footprint you would expect, derived from one stored coordinate.

    Useful consequence: if the anchor vessel is dead on but the error grows as the pipette works across the plate, the fault is the x / y pitch in equipment.yaml, not the point. Re-capturing the anchor will not fix it.

    0.3. What the stored Z means

    The anchor’s Z doesn’t define how deep the pipette goes into an item. It is the height the pipette sits at over that item, and the height it travels at to reach the item without clipping anything. Every working move is a relative drop from there, set per model by z-drop — and, on a tip rack, z-lift.

    z-drop is that drop: how far, in millimetres, the tip descends from the anchor Z to reach its working position on that item. It is stored in equipment.yaml rather than points.yaml because it describes the item itself — how far down inside it the tip has to go — not where the item sits on the deck. Move the same rack to the other side of the bed and its z-drop is unchanged; only its anchor moves.

    What counts as the working position depends on what the item is for, so the same key does three slightly different jobs:

    • Tip rack — far enough down onto the tip to seat it firmly.
    • Source — down to just below the surface of a full vessel.
    • Target — just inside the mouth of the well, before the plunger fires.

    Important: because the anchor Z doubles as the travel height over that item, z-drop must be at least the distance from the item’s rim down to its working point. Set the anchor too low and the handler will cross the deck at a height that drags the tip through neighbouring labware.

    1. Storing an anchor — points.yaml

    points.yaml is a flat list of named coordinates. Nothing in it knows what kind of equipment it belongs to — it is just a place on the deck:

    bosco-96-wellplate:
  x: 41.9
  y: 101.7
  z: 150.0
bosco-falcon-tubes:
  x: 156.9
  y: 206.6
  z: 249.5
small-tip-holder-bosco:
  x: 102.6
  y: 66.6
  z: 164.3

    The numbers are millimetres in the handler’s own coordinate frame — exactly what the Manual tab’s Set button reads back from the machine. They are only meaningful relative to the homing position, so the machine must be homed before any coordinate is captured or used.

    1.1. Capturing an anchor from the GUI

    This is the normal route, and it is written up step by step in Software §1.5, Calibrating equipment. In short: jog the tip to the anchor vessel, raise it by that model’s z-drop, then Set and Save. The GUI writes the coordinate straight into points.yaml under the name you give it.

    Raising by z-drop before saving is the part people skip. The stored Z is the height above the working point (see §0.3), so if you capture the position with the tip still down in the vessel, every run will drive the tip z-drop millimetres into the bottom of it.

    1.2. Editing points by hand

    Hand-editing is the quickest way to apply a correction you have measured. If a dry run shows the tip landing 0.4 mm short in Y across the whole plate, add 0.4 to that point’s y rather than re-jogging and re-capturing — it is repeatable and you can see exactly what changed.

    Keep the structure as the GUI writes it: two-space indentation, one key per point, and x, y, z beneath it. Plain numbers only, no units and no quotes.

    1.3. Naming points

    Names are free-form but must be unique, because preset.yaml refers to them by name. Including the handler is a good habit when you run more than one machine (bosco-96-wellplate, charlie-tip-holder), since the points file is shared across all of them.

    ⚠ Warning: if a preset names a point that does not exist — a typo, or a point deleted later — the equipment silently falls back to 0, 0, 0 instead of raising an error. The handler will then drive to the corner of its workspace. If a run heads for the corner, check the spelling of the origin: entries in preset.yaml first.

    2. Describing the hardware — equipment.yaml

    One entry per equipment model. This is what turns a single anchor into a full set of positions, and what tells the pipette how far to descend once it gets there.

    2.1. Field reference

    KeyUnitsApplies toWhat it does
    name—allLabel shown in the GUI; keep it the same as the entry key
    type—allList of roles this model may be assigned: Source, Target, TipHolder
    rows / columnscountallSize of the grid
    xmmallColumn pitch, centre to centre
    ymmallRow pitch, centre to centre
    origin—allWhich corner the anchor sits on (see §0.2); defaults to top-left
    z-dropmmallHow far the tip descends from the anchor Z to reach its working position
    z-liftmmTipHolderHow far to rise after seating a tip, to clear the rack
    liquid-heightmmSourceHeight of the liquid column when the vessel is full
    liquid-amountµLSourceVolume the vessel holds when full

    Important: the role a preset assigns must appear in that model’s type list. Assigning a model as a Source when its list only says ["TipHolder"] fails on load.

    2.2. Tip racks — picking up from the right hole

    small-tip-holder:
  name: small-tip-holder
  type:
  - TipHolder
  rows: 5
  columns: 11
  x: 9
  y: 9
  origin: top-right
  z-drop: 10
  z-lift: 61

    To collect a tip the handler runs a fixed four-move sequence:

    1. Travel to the chosen tip’s X/Y at the rack’s anchor Z.
    2. Descend z-drop at high feedrate, pressing the nozzle into the tip.
    3. Rise z-lift, carrying the seated tip up out of the rack.
    4. Park above the rack’s first position, ready to travel.

    The X/Y of each hole comes straight from the grid, so the pitch and the anchor corner are what decide whether the nozzle meets a tip or the plastic between tips. The printed racks use origin: top-right, so the grid steps in −X and −Y from the anchor — set the anchor on the rack’s top-right hole, not its bottom-left.

    Two depths then decide whether the tip actually stays on:

    • z-drop is the interference fit. Too little and the tip is not seated and drops off in transit; too much and the nozzle bottoms out — this move runs at a high feedrate, so driving into a hard stop can skip Z steps and cost you the coordinate frame. Start around 10 mm and adjust a millimetre at a time.
    • z-lift must exceed the tip’s length plus z-drop, or the fitted tip will still be inside the rack when the handler starts its next X/Y move and will be dragged across it. 61 mm suits the short printed racks; the taller tip-holder needs 95 mm.

    Capture the tip rack’s anchor with no tip fitted, nozzle just touching the top of the tip in the anchor hole, then raise by z-drop before saving.

    2.3. Sources — meeting the stock solution

    2-20ml-bottle-holder:
  name: 2-20ml-bottle-holder
  type:
  - Source
  - Target
  rows: 1
  columns: 2
  x: 33
  y: 24
  z-drop: 35
  liquid-height: 27
  liquid-amount: 18000

    A source is the one case where the descent is not fixed. The GUI tracks how much liquid it believes is left in each vessel, and works out the depth for every draw:

    current liquid height = liquid-height − (liquid-height ÷ liquid-amount × volume remaining)
total descent         = z-drop + current liquid height

    Which behaves like this:

    • Full vessel — the computed height is 0, so the tip descends exactly z-drop. That position should put it just below the surface.
    • Part-used vessel — as liquid is drawn the remaining volume falls, the computed height grows, and the tip descends further, chasing the meniscus down so it stays submerged without ever reaching into the bottom of the vessel.
    • Empty vessel — the GUI warns that the vessel may be empty and falls back to the full-vessel depth.

    So for a source, z-drop is the distance from the anchor Z down to just below the surface of a full vessel — which is exactly the position the Configure Plate procedure asks you to jog to before raising by z-drop and pressing Set.

    Important: liquid-height and liquid-amount must describe the same fill. If you declare 18 000 µL at 27 mm but only fill the bottle to 15 mm, the tracking starts from a surface that is not there and the tip will sit above the liquid, aspirating air, until enough volume has notionally been drawn.

    The running estimate is per session, not persisted. If you restart the GUI mid-plate, or refill a bottle, set the true starting volume by clicking the vessel in the plate layout, or use Reset Volume to return everything to full — see Software §2.2. Underestimating is safer than overestimating: it makes the tip go deeper, not shallower.

    2.4. Targets — dispensing into the well

    96-wellplate:
  name: 96-wellplate
  type:
  - Source
  - Target
  rows: 12
  columns: 8
  x: 9
  y: 9
  origin: bottom-left
  z-drop: 2
  liquid-height: 11
  liquid-amount: 350

    Dispensing runs in this order:

    1. Travel to the well’s X/Y. The handler compares the clearance height of the item it has just left with that of the target and moves X/Y first or Z first accordingly, so the tip passes over the taller of the two.
    2. Descend z-drop into the well.
    3. Fully depress the plunger, then dwell.
    4. Optionally dip — descend liquid-height and return — to shed any droplet still hanging on the tip.
    5. Rise z-drop back to the anchor Z and release the plunger.

    For a target, z-drop only has to get the tip inside the mouth of the well before the plunger fires, so the droplet cannot land on the rim or bridge two wells. Keep it small — 2 mm on a 96-well plate. Too deep and the tip strikes the bottom of the well; too shallow and the droplet hangs on the tip instead of transferring.

    ⚠ Important: a model listing both Source and Target uses the same z-drop in both roles — as a source it is measured to the liquid surface, as a target to the well mouth. Where one value cannot serve both, define the model twice under different names with different depths.

    The same applies to mixed labware: one entry describes one uniform vessel type. A rack holding tubes of different diameters or depths needs a separate entry per vessel type, as noted in Software §2.2.2.

    3. Binding the bed plate together — preset.yaml

    A preset is one bed-plate layout: it pairs each model with the anchor it stands on and the role it plays in a run.

    bosco-half-wellplate:
  200ul-tip-box-holder:
    equipment: 200ul-tip-box-holder
    origin: bosco-200ul-tip-box-holder
    type: TipHolder
  falcon-tube-holder:
    equipment: falcon-tube-holder
    origin: bosco-falcon-tubes
    type: Source
  half-96-wellplate:
    equipment: half-96-wellplate
    origin: bosco-96-wellplate
    type: Source
  half-96-wellplate1:
    equipment: half-96-wellplate
    origin: bosco-upper-96-wellplate
    type: Target
    • The top-level key is the preset name, as it appears in the Preset dropdown.
    • Each child key is a slot on the bed plate and must be unique within the preset.
    • equipment: names an entry in equipment.yaml.
    • origin: names an entry in points.yaml.
    • type: is the role for this slot, and must appear in that model’s type list.

    Note the two half-plates above: the same model, on two different anchors, in two different roles. That is why the slot key and the equipment: value are separate fields — the second slot is named half-96-wellplate1 so the two do not collide.

    Finally, a handler loads a preset on startup through its own entry in printers.yaml:

    bosco:
  max_vol: 100
  min_vol: 10
  preset: bosco-half-wellplate
  # ...

    Important: any time you edit printers.yaml you must close and re-open the GUI for the changes to take effect. After hand-editing any of the other config files, restart as well — it is the only way to be sure the GUI is not still holding the values it loaded earlier.

    4. Checking the calibration

    4.1. Dry run

    Never prove a calibration with liquid in the vessels. Work through it dry, with the workspace clear enough that you can stop the machine:

    1. Home all axes. Coordinates mean nothing until the machine is homed, and an unhomed Move can drive into the frame.
    2. In Configure Plate, select each item in turn and press Move. The tip should arrive centred over the anchor vessel — the one marked with the red dot on the layout map.
    3. Check the far corner too, by running a short assignment that targets the last well of each item. The anchor being right does not mean the pitch is.
    4. Watch the plate layout for overlapping grids. Two items clipping into each other on screen means they will collide in reality.
    5. Run the full protocol dry — no tips, empty vessels — and watch each descent before you trust it with reagent.

    With the geometry confirmed, calibrate the delivered volume against a scale: §6 below.

    5. Calibrating the tip ejection travel — printers.yaml

    HEIMDALL doesn’t have a separate motor for ejecting tips. Instead, it uses a fixed bar bolted to the tip disposal chamber — the same part you installed in Assembly → Tip Ejector.

    The idea is quite simple: rather than having something push the ejection mechanism to push the tip off, the machine uses the gantry’s own movement to do the job. The gantry catches the pipette’s ejector behind the fixed bar and moves the pipette up along the Z-axis, pushing the pipette upwards towards the ejector and stripping the tip off.

    That makes the ejection distances geometry, not timing, and it makes them unforgiving in both directions: too little travel and the tip never leaves, too much and the gantry keeps pushing after the ejector has bottomed out.

    5.1. The five moves

    The whole sequence lives in printers.yaml and runs as relative moves from a captured start coordinate. Software §1.4 shows the sequence and walks through capturing it; this section is about getting the two Z values right.

    ValueMoveWhat it decides
    dispose_start—The coordinate the sequence is captured against — set by walking the sequence in reverse, Software §1.4
    dispose_initial_yYBrings the pipette across to the ejector bar
    dispose_initial_zZThe approach height — puts the pipette’s ejector level with the bar so it can engage
    dispose_yYSlides the ejector into engagement with the bar
    dispose_actuate_zZThe actuation travel — the stroke that presses the ejector and releases the tip

    ⚠ Warning: Before calibrating, make sure a tip is fitted but contains no liquid. Keep your hand close to the power switch so you can stop the machine immediately if something goes wrong.

    The main risk is that the gantry may keep pushing even when something is stuck or can no longer move. Since the Z-axis won’t necessarily stop on its own, make small adjustments and watch how the mechanism responds. If you hear the motor straining, grinding or skipping steps, stop immediately and check what’s obstructing the movement before continuing.

    5.2. The approach height — dispose_initial_z

    This is the one that decides whether the ejector engages the bar at all. The pipette has to end up at the height where its ejector will pass under the bar when the next Y move slides it across — too high and it misses entirely, too low and the pipette body or the bracket fouls the bar as it translates.

    1. Home all axes, then jog to dispose_start so you start where the sequence starts.
    2. Apply the Y move by hand with the D-pad, so the pipette sits across from the bar.
    3. Jog down in Z a millimetre at a time, watching from the side, until the pipette’s ejector is level with the part of the bar that will press it. Watch the pipette, not the screen.
    4. Check the clearance before committing. From that height, jog the second Y move by hand in small steps. The ejector should slide into engagement with nothing else touching. If anything scrapes, come back up and try again a little higher or lower.
    5. Record the Z difference from step 1 to step 3 as dispose_initial_z.

    Follow the sign convention already in your printers.yaml rather than assuming one — these are relative moves, and whether a descent is written positive or negative depends on how the file is set up. The safest check is to jog the move manually first and confirm the pipette goes the way you expect.

    5.3. The actuation travel — dispose_actuate_z

    With the ejector engaged, this is the stroke that does the work. Your pipette’s ejector has a fixed amount of travel built into it, and somewhere inside that travel is the point where the tip lets go. You want to clear that point and stop — not run to the end of the mechanism.

    1. Find the mechanism’s limit first. Off the machine, press the pipette’s ejector by hand through its full stroke and measure it with calipers. That measurement is a ceiling you must stay under, not a target.
    2. Start short. Set dispose_actuate_z to roughly half that stroke, restart the GUI, fit a tip and run the disposal sequence.
    3. Increase in 0.5 mm steps until the tip releases cleanly, restarting the GUI after each edit. Expect the tip to be reluctant and then suddenly free — there is no gradual middle.
    4. Add a small margin — about a millimetre past the first value that works — so a slightly stiff tip still releases. Stop there.
    5. Confirm you are not over-driving. Run the sequence and listen. A clean ejection is quiet; a grinding or buzzing Z axis means the ejector has bottomed out and the motor is skipping steps. Then home the machine and check that Z still arrives where it used to — if it has shifted, you have been losing steps and the value is too large.

    Why the margin has a ceiling: once the pipette’s ejector reaches its internal stop, the next millimetre of travel goes into the printed ejector, the pipette bracket and the Z screw instead. It will usually show up first as lost Z position, and later as a cracked printed part. “It ejects more reliably” is not a reason to keep increasing this number.

    5.4. Verifying the ejection

    One successful ejection proves very little — tips vary, and the failure you care about is the intermittent one that drops a tip on the deck halfway through an overnight run.

    1. Ten in a row. Pick up and eject ten tips without touching anything. Every one should land in the chamber.
    2. Every tip type you use. A 1000 µL tip and a 200 µL tip do not seat with the same force or release at the same point. If you run both, both have to pass.
    3. Watch where they land. Tips that release but miss the chamber are a Y problem — dispose_y and dispose_initial_y — not a height problem.
    4. Home and re-check afterwards. Run the full sequence ten times, then home and move to a known point. If it no longer lands where it did, the actuation travel is over-driving the axis.

    Re-check after anything that changes the geometry: re-seating the pipette in its bracket, reprinting the ejector or disposal chamber, moving the chamber on the bed plate, or switching tip brand. The approach height in particular depends on exactly how high the pipette sits in its bracket.

    6. Calibrating the dispense volume — printers.yaml

    Everything up to here gets the tip to the right place. This section is about how much liquid leaves it. The two are calibrated independently, and a handler can be excellent at one and useless at the other.

    Two separate numbers decide the delivered volume:

    • What the pipette itself is set to — the GUI drives the pipette’s own volume dial and reads the display back through the camera. That side is set up in Software §1.1–1.3: min_vol, max_vol, decimal_position and vol_adjust_position.
    • How long the actuator works the plunger — aspiration_actuation_time in printers.yaml. That is what you calibrate on this page.

    The plunger stroke is open-loop. The actuator runs for a calculated length of time and nothing measures how far the plunger actually travelled. Friction in the actuator, the plunger force of your particular pipette, the tip you fit and the actuator’s own speed all shift the relationship between that time and the microlitres that come out, and none of it can be derived from the CAD. The pipette expels what it drew, so the only ground truth is the mass of the liquid that lands in the vessel.

    You calibrate by comparison: pipette the target volume by hand with the same pipette, let the machine pipette the same volume, weigh both, and change the timing until the machine matches. Working mass against mass means the density of the water never enters the arithmetic — both sides are the same liquid at the same temperature, so it cancels. There is nothing to convert and no thermometer to read.

    The hand-pipetted vessels are the reference, so the machine ends up as accurate as the pipette driving it — no better. If the pipette itself is out of specification, have it serviced before you calibrate anything against it.

    6.1. What you need

    • An analytical balance reading to 0.1 mg, on a solid bench, levelled, with its draught shield in use. For volumes below about 10 µL you want 0.01 mg.
    • Deionised or distilled water from a single container, left on the bench long enough to stop changing temperature. Both the hand reference and the machine run must be drawn from that same container.
    • Twelve clean, dry target vessels of the type you actually run — six for the hand reference and six for the machine — plus forceps or gloves. Fingerprints weigh roughly a tenth of a milligram.
    • Tips of the brand and size you will use in real protocols. A different tip is a different calibration.

    Before you start: finish the geometry first. If the tip is not centred in the well or sits at the wrong depth, you will be weighing splashes and droplets left on the rim, and no amount of timing adjustment will make the numbers settle.

    6.2. The gravimetric loop

    The run itself — weigh the empty vessels, take the hand reference, dispense the same volume with the machine, weigh again, adjust and repeat — is written up step by step in Software §1.7. Work through it there; it is a loop, so expect three or four passes before the numbers sit where you want them. What follows is what that procedure does not tell you: how to keep the weights trustworthy, and how far to move the timing once you have them.

    Keeping the weights trustworthy. Five things decide whether the numbers are worth acting on at all:

    • Prime before the first draw. Use Prime in the Manual tab to fill the fluid path and clear air bubbles. An unprimed system reads low and scatters badly.
    • Pre-wet the tip. Aspirate the target volume and dispense it back into the source two or three times. A dry tip loses part of the first draw to wetting its own wall, which shows up as one anomalously light vessel.
    • Weigh all twelve vessels individually — not a single tare — and keep them in a known order, so each mass belongs to a known position.
    • Leave the volume dial alone between the two sides. Set it once, let Adjust Volume drive the dial and confirm the reading through the camera, then use that identical setting for the hand reference and the machine run.
    • Weigh each vessel immediately after filling it. Small volumes evaporate while you queue at the balance, so weigh as you go rather than all at the end.

    Reading the two means. Accuracy is how far the machine sits from the hand reference; precision is the spread within the machine’s own six. Report both — they fail for different reasons:

    error = (m_machine − m_hand) / m_hand × 100 %
CV    = SD(m_machine) / m_machine × 100 %

    Both are masses in mg, straight off the balance — nothing to convert, and no temperature to know, because the same water sits on both sides of the comparison. A large error means the actuation time is wrong. A large CV means the handler is not repeatable yet; adjusting the timing will only move the scatter around, so chase the cause in §6.5 first.

    How far to move the timing. The ESP runs the linear actuator for a calculated length of time, and aspiration_actuation_time in printers.yaml is the number that calculation starts from — so that one value is the whole adjustment. Dispensing light means the actuator needs longer; heavy means shorter. Scale it by the ratio of the two masses:

    new time ≈ old time × m_hand / m_machine

    Treat that as a starting point rather than a solution. The stroke is not perfectly proportional to time — the plunger has to overcome static friction before it moves at all — so the first correction usually overshoots slightly on small volumes. One good run is luck; two in a row is a calibration.

    6.3. Calibrate at the volumes you actually use

    One actuation time has to serve the whole working range, and because the stroke is not strictly linear in time, a setting tuned at 1000 µL can be noticeably off at 50 µL. Check at least three points — near min_vol, mid-range, and near max_vol — and record the error at each. Each point needs its own hand reference: pipette that volume by hand, weigh it, and compare the machine against that mass. A reference taken at one volume says nothing about another.

    If no single value satisfies every point, favour the volume your protocols actually transfer most often and accept the error elsewhere, or split the work across two handler entries in printers.yaml with different timings. Do not average the error across a range you never use.

    6.4. What quietly ruins a gravimetric calibration

    Nearly every “the machine will not calibrate” case is one of these rather than a bad actuation time:

    • Evaporation. At 10 µL, a vessel left open on the bench can lose a measurable fraction while you weigh the others. Weigh immediately, cap if you can, and keep the draught shield closed.
    • A dry tip. Not pre-wetting biases the first dispense low every time.
    • Air in the path. Bubbles compress, so part of the plunger stroke goes into the bubble instead of the liquid. Re-prime and watch the tip.
    • Static. Plastic vessels on a dry day drift on the balance by tenths of a milligram. Let the reading settle, or use glass.
    • Water from two different containers. The comparison only cancels density if both sides are the same liquid at the same temperature. Draw the hand reference and the machine run from one container, in one sitting.
    • A rushed hand reference. The machine is being matched to it, so a sloppy manual draw — wrong angle, released too fast, tip not pre-wetted — becomes the machine’s error. Pipette those six as carefully as you would for an assay.
    • Vibration and airflow. A balance next to a running handler or under an air vent will not settle. Weigh with the handler idle.
    • Changing anything afterwards. A new tip brand, a different pipette, a re-tensioned or replaced actuator, or a rebuilt carriage all invalidate the number. Re-run the loop.

    Record what you settled on — date, handler, pipette, tip, the volumes you checked, the hand-reference mass and the error at each, and the final aspiration_actuation_time. It is the only way to tell later whether the machine has drifted or has always been like that, and it turns the next re-check into a ten-minute job.
